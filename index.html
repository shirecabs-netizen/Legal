1Proposed claim for judicial review

1.1Decisions challenged

I propose to apply to the Administrative Court in Wales for permission to bring a claim for judicial review, and thereafter for substantive relief, in respect of:

(i)
The inspection of hackney carriage vehicle DG66 YLA on 6 October 2026, purportedly conducted pursuant to section 68 of the Local Government (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1976 (“the 1976 Act”);
(ii)
The purported decision of Monmouthshire County Council (“the Council”), taken through its Licensing Officer, Mr Taylor J Watts, on 6 October 2026, to suspend hackney carriage vehicle licence HV0053;
(iii)
The Council’s purported decisions of 7 and 19 October 2026 to continue the said suspension;
(iv)
The procedure prescribed by the Council for the termination of the suspension;
(v)
The Council’s continuing refusal to give effect to the certificate of an approved testing station dated 8 October 2026, and its requirement that the vehicle be presented again for inspection; and
(vi)
The cumulative effect of the above, the effect of which is that no lawful route to the resolution of the suspension is available to me.

1.2Summary of the grounds

The grounds fall into two parts: Part A (grounds 1 to 4), lawful authority and the notice; and Part B (ground 5), the certificate of an approved testing station. In summary:

1.
Mr Watts purported to inspect and to suspend as an authorised officer. The Council’s published records, including its scheme of delegation, disclose no written authorisation, for the purposes of Part II of the 1976 Act, in favour of him or of any other officer, as section 68 requires and as section 80(1) and the Council’s own Policy define.
2.
The power to suspend, like the power to inspect, is exercisable only by an authorised officer or a constable, and none has been disclosed. Further or alternatively, under the Council’s own scheme of delegation the suspension of a hackney carriage vehicle licence is a function of the Taxi and Regulatory Committee, and the Constitution delegates that function to no officer.
3.
The route prescribed for the lifting of the suspension is unavailable in law. Under section 68 a suspension is terminated only upon the satisfaction of an authorised officer or constable, and the Council’s published documents identify none. Under the Council’s own scheme the suspension of licences is a function of the Taxi and Regulatory Committee, to which none of the Council’s communications refers.
4.
The notice and the Council’s subsequent communications misstate the statutory decision-maker, the statutory consequence and the statutory test, and treat an asserted inability to verify compliance as if it were a finding of unfitness. Those errors constitute a misdirection in law in their own right and, in addition, deprive Mr Watts’s bare assertion of authorised-officer status of any material weight.
5.
The Council has refused to give effect to the certificate of an approved testing station dated 8 October 2026, which its own Policy, statements and practice treat as the means by which fitness and compliance are assessed and confirmed, and has given no reason for disregarding it.

Grounds 1 to 4, taken together, are threshold grounds. They go to whether the statutory powers of inspection and suspension were exercised by a person possessing lawful authority, and to whether the Council has identified any lawful decision-maker competent to bring the suspension to an end. Ground 5 is advanced independently of them and applies whichever way they are resolved. Until those questions are resolved, I remain unable to work under licence HV0053.

1.3Primary and threshold grounds

The grounds advanced in this letter are the primary grounds of challenge. They are not, and are not intended to be, an exhaustive statement of the grounds of any claim filed in the Administrative Court, in particular because I act in person and have not received legal advice. I reserve the right to refine, amend or add to them, as set out in section 15.

7Grounds of challenge

The inspection, the suspension, its continuation and the prescribed route to lifting it are unlawful for the reasons set out below. The grounds fall into two parts. Part A (Grounds 1 to 4) concerns whether the inspection and suspension were exercised by a person with lawful authority and upon a lawful notice, and draws upon the Legal Test (paragraphs 7(A)(i) to 7(A)(v)), which is common to Grounds 1 to 3. Part B (Ground 5) concerns whether, even if that authority were assumed, there is any lawful basis for the continued refusal to give effect to the certificate of an approved testing station; it has its own legal test (paragraphs 7(B)(i) to 7(B)(v)), which draws also upon paragraphs 7(A)(ii), 7(A)(iii) and 7(A)(iv). Each ground nonetheless raises a distinct issue and is advanced independently of, and without prejudice to, the others.

PART ALawful authority to inspect and to suspend, the route to reinstatement, and the weight to be attached to the officer’s assertion of authority (Grounds 1 to 4)

The Legal Test

7(A)(i)
Section 68 of the 1976 Act confers the powers to inspect and test a licensed vehicle, to require its presentation for further inspection and testing, and to suspend its licence upon:
“any authorised officer of the council in question or any constable”.

Section 80(1) defines “authorised officer” in the following terms:

“any officer of a district council authorised in writing by the council for the purposes of this Part of this Act”.

By virtue of section 80(4), references to a district council are to be construed, in relation to Wales, as references to a county council or county borough council. Accordingly, an officer’s exercise of the powers conferred by section 68 depends upon written authorisation by the Council for the purposes of Part II of the 1976 Act. The existence and scope of that authorisation are therefore preliminary questions of statutory authority.

7(A)(ii)
The Council’s Taxi and Private Hire Policy 2026 (“the Policy”) expressly links authorised-officer status to the Scheme of Delegation contained in its published Constitution. The Policy therefore identifies the constitutional scheme as the source of the authority upon which its officers purport to act. At page 8, “Authorised Council Officer” is defined as:
“any officer of the Council authorised under the Council’s Scheme of Delegation as contained in the Constitution”.

That definition expressly directs the reader to the Council’s constitutional Scheme of Delegation. The same position is reinforced at page 9, which states that the Constitution:

“details who is responsible for making decisions on matters relating to taxi licensing” (from which it follows that responsibility for each decision is to be specified clearly and without ambiguity)

Page 9 further states that the Council:

“operates a scheme of delegation where certain powers are delegated to Authorised Officers and Elected Members who sit on the Taxi & Regulatory Committee”.

The Policy then confirms the availability of the governing instrument:

“A copy of the Constitution which contains the scheme of delegation is available on the Council’s website or upon request.”
7(A)(iii)
The Council has published the rules by which it regulates, and has recorded that it has had regard to the Regulators’ Code (April 2014) (“the Code”). At page 8, the Policy records that consideration has been given to the “Regulators Code 2014”, and at paragraph 4.8 the Council’s Investigation Report records that the investigation had regard to the “Regulators' Code” (Enclosures C, D and J).

On publication, the Code provides, at paragraph 6.1:

“Regulators should publish a set of clear service standards, setting out what those they regulate should expect from them.”

Those standards are to include, at paragraph 6.2(c) and (d):

“their approach to checks on compliance, including details of the risk assessment framework used to target those checks as well as protocols for their conduct, clearly setting out what those they regulate should expect”;
“their enforcement policy, explaining how they respond to non-compliance”.

Checks on compliance include inspections (footnote 6 to the Code). Paragraph 6.3 provides that the information is to be:

“easily accessible, including being available at a single point … on the regulator’s website that is clearly signposted, and it should be kept up to date”.

and paragraph 6.4 that:

“Regulators should have mechanisms in place to ensure that their officers act in accordance with their published service standards, including their enforcement policy.”

The Policy gives corresponding undertakings. At page 9 it states that, in exercising its discretion in carrying out its functions:

“the Licensing Authority will have regard to this Policy and the objectives set out therein”.

and that where it is necessary to depart significantly from the Policy:

“clear and substantive reasons for doing so will be given”.
7(A)(iv)
The Constitution identifies Part 3 as the governing record of the allocation of functions and decision-making authority. Clause 2.1.3 (Part 1, Introduction) provides:
“You can get a better understanding of what each of the Council’s bodies do in the Articles of the Constitution at Part 2, including Full Council, Cabinet, Scrutiny Committees, Standards Committee and Regulatory Committees. Section 14 provides information on the management and Officer structures of the Council. Some Officers have a specific duty to ensure that the Council operates within the law and uses resources wisely. Responsibility for functions is at Part 3 and details which Council bodies, and which Officers, have authority to make which decisions.”

Within the provisions of the Constitution that I have examined, Part 3 is named as the record of decision-making responsibility on eight occasions, across seven clauses: clause 2.1.3 (above); clause 6.6.1, under which the Council “will maintain the tables in Part 3 of this Constitution setting out the responsibilities for the Council’s functions which are not the responsibility of the executive”; clause 13.2.5, under which delegations to joint committees “will be found in the Council’s scheme of delegations in Part 3 of this Constitution”; clause 15.1.1, which records that the Council’s record of “what part of the Council or individual has responsibility for particular types of decisions” is “set out in Part 3 of this Constitution”; clause 19.1.1, which states that Part 3 “is designed to set out who is responsible for making decisions in Monmouthshire County Council”; clause 19.2.2, which states that keeping that record “is the purpose of Part 3” (see below); and clause 21.1.1, which twice refers, in the terms of reference of the Taxi and Regulatory Committee, to matters delegated separately under Part 3 of the Council’s Constitution.

On the Council’s own published and established account, Part 3 is therefore the single designated record of which Council bodies, and which officers, have authority to make which decisions, and that record is required to be detailed. If written authority permitting Mr Watts, or any other officer, to exercise the inspection, testing and suspension powers conferred by section 68 of the 1976 Act existed, it is in Part 3 that, on the Council’s own account, it would be recorded, and recorded in detail.

Hackney carriage and private hire licensing falls within the portfolio of the Strategic Director Social Care and Health, the Chief Officer responsible for that function. Any relevant officer delegation would accordingly be expected to appear among the delegations to that post.

Clause 26.5 of Part 3 sets out the delegations to the Strategic Director Social Care and Health. These include the licensing provisions at clauses 26.5.3(d) and (f), together with the provisions concerning other regulatory and licensing functions at clause 26.5.4(c).

Clause 26.5 is a detailed schedule comprising some 200 separately lettered or numbered entries over more than 18 pages, the greater part of which delegates a specified function or power under an identified enactment. Where the Council intends to confer power to authorise officers for the purposes of a particular enactment, the schedule makes express provision. Examples include:

“to authorise officers generally or specially for the purposes of the Food Safety Act 1990” (clause 26.5.4(a)(viii));
“to authorise officers under the Dangerous Dogs Act 1991” (clause 26.5.4(a)(xvii)); and
“to authorise officers under Part II of the Clean Neighbourhoods & Environment Act 2005” (clause 26.5.4(a)(xxiv)).

In relation to hackney carriage and private hire licensing, however, the only officer delegation I have identified in the Constitution is clause 26.5.4(c)(ii), which provides:

“in relation to the hackney carriage and private hire licensing functions, to approve applications for drivers’ licences where they are unproblematic and/or reveal minor traffic offences and/or the applicants had not received any additional convictions/endorsements since the previous licences were issued”.

The delegation is expressly confined to the approval of the specified categories of applications for drivers’ licences. On any construction, whether narrow or broad, it does not extend to the inspection or testing of vehicles, or to the suspension of a vehicle licence under section 68.

The inspection, suspension and prescribed route to reinstatement were each purportedly undertaken under section 68, and the notice adopts the terminology of the Policy. The Policy defines authorised-officer status by reference to the Constitution; clause 2.1.3 directs the reader to Part 3; and, on my examination of that Part, no provision has been identified authorising an officer to exercise the powers in section 68. The sole officer delegation I have identified in this field concerns the approval of the specified applications for drivers’ licences.

The statements at paragraph 7(A)(ii) and in paragraph 7(A)(iii) are made by the Council to every licence holder. They are clear and unambiguous, and they address the very question that arises here: who may exercise the powers of inspection and suspension, how the Council’s approach to checks is to be published, and what the Council will do if it departs from what it has published. A person who holds a licence granted by the Council is entitled to expect that the Council will follow its own published procedure, and will not depart from it without first identifying the departure and giving clear reasons for it. That is a legitimate expectation of the kind recognised and protected in public law. It is distinct from, and additional to, the statutory requirement that an authorised officer be authorised in writing, and it operates whichever way the Council characterises the power.

The Council’s own rules for the exercise of delegated powers point the same way. Clause 19.2.2 of the Constitution provides:

“The Council must issue and keep up to date a record of which individual has responsibility for particular types of decisions. This is the purpose of Part 3.”

Clause 24.4.3 makes any exercise of delegated powers subject to “any statutory restrictions” and “any other policies”, and clause 24.4.4(c) provides that, in exercising delegated powers, officers shall:

“maintain a full record of the exercise of those powers, including all relevant details, in accordance with any corporate guidance given by the Monitoring Officer”.

Measured against those rules, the Council has not followed its own procedure, and it cannot rely upon an unpublished instrument to supply what its published standards omit. First, the Scheme of Delegation to which the Policy refers discloses no written authorisation of Mr Watts, or of any officer, to inspect or to suspend (paragraph 7(A)(iv)). Secondly, I have identified no protocol for the conduct of inspections, and no statement of which officers may conduct them or decide upon a suspension, in any standard or policy published under paragraphs 6.1 to 6.3 of the Code. Thirdly, the notice does not state that the Council has departed from the Policy and gives no reasons for any departure, and no record has been identified of any decision that a provision of the Code is inapplicable or outweighed. Fourthly, no record of the exercise of the power in accordance with clause 24.4.4(c) has been identified.

7(A)(v)
The suspension notice relies upon the following assertion of statutory authority:
“I, Taylor Watts being an Authorised Officer of Monmouthshire County Council”.

An assertion of authorised-officer status does not, of itself, establish the existence of the written authorisation required by section 80(1) of the 1976 Act and by the Council’s Policy, which governs my licensed activity.

In the premises, I do not accept the authority asserted in the notice because, on the Council’s own Policy and procedures, none exists. The statute requires an officer to be authorised in writing (paragraph 7(A)(i)); the Policy locates that authorisation in the Scheme of Delegation contained in the Constitution (paragraph 7(A)(ii)); the Council’s published standards under the Code identify no officer authorised to inspect or to suspend (paragraph 7(A)(iii)); and Part 3, which the Constitution repeatedly designates as the record of who may take which decisions, contains no such officer authorisation (paragraph 7(A)(iv)).

GROUND 1No authorisation published for Mr Watts or any Council Officer to inspect and test HV0053
7(A)(vi)
The power to inspect and test is conferred upon an authorised officer or a constable, and upon no other person (paragraph 7(A)(i)). Mr Watts is not a constable. His power to inspect DG66 YLA on 6 October 2026 accordingly depended entirely upon his holding an authorisation from the Council for the purposes of Part II of the 1976 Act, that being the status which section 80(1) and the Council’s own established Policy define as that of an authorised officer. The Council’s published documents, examined in the Legal Test (paragraphs 7(A)(v) to 7(A)(v)), disclose none.
7(A)(vii)
It follows that the asserted inspection cannot be an inspection under section 68 at all. An inspection carried out by an officer whose authorisation cannot be established is incapable of founding the dissatisfaction as to fitness upon which the further powers in section 68 depend, and everything done by the Council in consequence, including the notice of 6 October 2026 and the decisions to continue the suspension, falls with it.
7(A)(viii)
The bare assertion of authorised-officer status in the notice (paragraph 7(A)(v)) is a label; it is not the authorisation by reference to which section 80(1) and the Council’s own Policy Framework define an authorised officer. The reliability of the assertion is addressed under Ground 4. The purported inspection is, moreover, inconsistent with the Policy, which locates authorised-officer status in the Council’s Scheme of Delegation (paragraph 7(A)(ii)) and which requires it to be set out in detail.
GROUND 2No established and published policy to suspend licence HV0053
7(A)(ix)
The power to suspend a vehicle licence under section 68 is, like the power to inspect, conferred only upon an authorised officer or a constable. In the absence of an authorisation from the Council, that being the status which section 80(1) and the Council’s own Policy define as that of an authorised officer (Ground 1), Mr Watts had no authority to suspend licence HV0053, and the purported suspension fails upon that basis alone. This ground additionally raises a separate question, howsoever the power is characterised. If it is a power conferred directly upon the authorised officer, it depends upon the written authorisation which the Policy locates in the Constitution (paragraph 7(A)(ii)) and which has not been identified (paragraph 7(A)(iv)). If it is a function of the Council, to be discharged through the Council’s arrangements, the question is whether the Council’s Constitution and scheme of delegation allocated the decision to suspend a hackney carriage vehicle licence to any officer. The scheme operates, in the words of clause 24.1.3 of the Constitution:
“under Sections 101, 151 and 270 of the Local Government Act 1972”.

Section 101(1) of that Act permits a local authority to:

“arrange for the discharge of any of their functions … by a committee, a sub-committee or an officer of the authority”.

As set out in the Legal Test (paragraph 7(A)(iv)), the only officer delegation that I have identified in this field is clause 26.5.4(c)(ii), which is confined to the approval of specified applications for drivers’ licences and does not extend to any other authorisation.

The Constitution does, however, expressly allocate the functions of suspending, revoking and cancelling licences. Clause 21.1.1, replicated in Annex 2, assigns those functions to the Taxi and Regulatory Committee in the following terms:

“To discharge on behalf of the Council all matters relating to the grant, refusal, issue, revocation, cancellation and suspension of licences or permits and without prejudice to the generality of the foregoing to discharge these functions.”

The operative words, “to discharge”, allocate the function to the Committee. I have identified no arrangement under section 101 of the Local Government Act 1972 by which the Committee has caused an officer to discharge that function in respect of the suspension of a vehicle licence. Had the Committee made any such arrangement, it would be recorded, in detail, in Part 3 of the Constitution.

If the Council contends that the section 68 power is vested directly in the authorised officer, the Policy requires that officer’s authority to appear in the Scheme of Delegation (paragraph 7(A)(ii)), and it does not (paragraph 7(A)(iv)). If it contends that the power is a function of the Council, the published scheme assigns that function to the Committee (clause 21.1.1, above), and the delegations which the Constitution contains, including its general provisions, do not extend to the suspension of a hackney carriage vehicle licence under section 68. The notice, the email of 7 October 2026 and the letter of 19 October 2026 identified no delegation, and none has been published (paragraphs 7(A)(iii) and 7(A)(iv)). A decision taken by an officer who held no power to take it is ultra vires and liable to be quashed.

GROUND 3No lawful route to lifting the suspension provided: section 68 empowers only an authorised officer or a constable and not “the Authority”
7(A)(x)
The Council has not identified a lawful route to the lifting of the suspension. Its notice and later communications demanded rectification and presentation of the vehicle for further inspection; the letter of 19 October 2026 also refers to an “inspection and test” that may identify further defects, and directs the delivery of the licence plates to County Hall. Yet section 68 ends a suspension only when “such authorised officer or constable is so satisfied” “as to the fitness of the hackney carriage or private hire vehicle”, and an officer must be authorised in writing as section 80(1) and the Council’s own Policy define (paragraphs 7(A)(i) to 7(A)(v)). The Council’s published Constitution identifies neither an officer authorised to conduct that inspection nor one empowered to give the required statutory satisfaction. Further, I contend that its scheme allocates the suspension, revocation and cancellation of licences to the Taxi and Regulatory Committee, without delegating that function to an officer (clause 21.1.1; paragraph 7(A)(ix)). None of the Council’s communications identifies any role for the Committee or for a Sub-Committee acting on its behalf. Since the Council identifies no lawful (authorised) decision-maker and no authority relied upon, repetition of the prescribed process leaves me in a legal limbo – The Constitution authorises no officer to whom the vehicle could be presented for further inspection and testing; and, whereas the Constitution allocates the suspension of licences to the Committee, section 68 confers the power to bring a suspension to an end upon an authorised officer or constable alone. I am accordingly left without any lawful route by which the suspension may be terminated.
GROUND 4Misdirection on the face of the notice, the weight of the officer’s assertion of authority and the reliability of the officer’s ability to carry out a statutory duty
7(A)(xi)
This ground is advanced independently, and it also bears upon Grounds 1 and 2. The Council may invite the court to presume that Mr Watts acted with proper authority. Any presumption that official acts were regularly performed is rebuttable, and carries little weight where the instrument relied upon misstates the very provision under which it is issued, as the notice does in three respects. First, the notice substitutes the “Licensing Authority” for the statutory decision-maker. It states that the suspension:
“shall remain in force until the defect has been rectified, the vehicle has been presented for inspection, and the Licensing Authority is satisfied that the vehicle complies with all licence conditions”.

That is incorrect. Under section 68 the suspension continues until the authorised officer or constable is so satisfied (paragraph 7(A)(x)).

The statute identifies the authorised officer or constable whose satisfaction brings the suspension to an end; the notice instead ascribes that role to the “Licensing Authority”. Mr Watts’s email of 6 October 2026 repeats that formulation. The substitution is material because it removes entirely the authorised officer upon whom the statute depends.

7(A)(xii)
Secondly, the notice converts the statutory consequence from “shall” to “may”. It states that, if the authorised officer is not satisfied before the expiry of two months, the vehicle licence:
“may be deemed revoked”.

However, section 68 provides that the licence:

“shall, by virtue of this section, be deemed to have been revoked”.

I recognise that an inaccurate description does not of itself necessarily invalidate an otherwise lawful notice. The distinction is nevertheless material, because the notice purports to explain the consequence of failing to obtain the required statutory satisfaction within two months, and it presents a mandatory consequence as an optional one. That misstatement of a mandatory statutory consequence calls into question whether the notice was prepared with proper regard to the terms of section 68.

7(A)(xiii)
Thirdly, following from the above paragraphs 7(A)(xi) and 7(A)(xii), the Officer reverts from the satisfaction of “the Authority” to that of the “authorised Officer”, an inconsistency which further evidences the confusion attending the decision-making process.
7(A)(xiv)
Fourthly, in his email of 7 October 2026, Mr Watts referred to “the Authority’s ability to verify compliance”. I was present throughout the encounter and did not prevent the officers from inspecting the vehicle. An inability to verify compliance is not, without more, a finding that the vehicle was unfit for use as a hackney carriage. The email identifies neither the inspection or test attempted nor the matters which could not be verified, and does not explain how the statutory threshold for suspension was met. It is further to be noted that Mr Watts repeatedly stated that I was required to satisfy “the Authority”, whereas the statute refers to “an authorised officer”.
PART BThe certificate of fitness (Ground 5)

The Legal Test for Ground 5

7(B)(i)
The question which section 68 directs to the authorised officer’s satisfaction is fitness. Where the officer “is not satisfied as to the fitness of the hackney carriage or private hire vehicle”, he may require the proprietor to make it available for further inspection and testing, and may suspend the licence “until such time as such authorised officer or constable is so satisfied”. The notice records that Mr Watts was “not satisfied as to the fitness of the vehicle” by reason of one matter only, namely the wording displayed on the rooflight. It then substitutes a different test for the lifting of the suspension, namely that “the Licensing Authority is satisfied that the vehicle complies with all licence conditions” (paragraph 7(A)(xi)). The question to be answered before the suspension can end remains the statutory one: whether the vehicle is fit.
7(B)(ii)
The Council has published the means by which that question is answered. At page 28 the Policy states:
“Vehicle testing arrangements are currently through a nominated approved testing station at which compliance with the requirements is assessed and confirmed by the issue of a certificate.”

At pages 28 and 29 it provides that the exemption from the requirement of an MOT certificate applies only:

“following submission of the Certificate of Testing by an approved Testing Station authorised by MCC and the issue of the licence”.

The Council’s Taxi and Private Hire Newsletter of 7 January 2026 advised licence holders to amend the roof sign “before the vehicle is next tested as the approved garage may fail a licensed vehicle on this aspect”. As I understand it, the approved testing station arrangement was established by a decision of the Council’s elected members at a meeting of the Licensing and Regulatory Committee in 2022, replacing the former arrangements with individual garages. That decision is not published with the Policy, and the Council is asked at section 11 to identify it. The Council has, on its own published account, authorised the approved testing station to assess and confirm compliance, and it issues licences upon that confirmation.

7(B)(iii)
The Council has also made specific assertions as to what the approved testing station’s test assesses, and from when. By letter of 29 July 2026 (ref D087 / HV0053), Ms Lisa Griffin, Head of Housing & Public Protection, wrote that:
“I am advised the vehicle inspection at the garage would not have assessed compliance against the new 2026 Policy arrangements as these requirements would take effect from the date of the issue of your new licence following the renewal application. Therefore, your vehicle was subject to inspection against the 2023 Policy arrangements.”

The renewed licence was issued in June 2026 (email of Mr Watts, 4 June 2026). Mr Watts told the Council’s investigator (Investigation Report, p.13) that:

“His vehicle was tested earlier in April 26 under the old policy, but by the time his vehicle is tested in October 26 he needs to comply with the new policy”.

It follows that the officer (even assuming, contrary to my primary case, that his authority could be established) had available to him the means of being satisfied as to the fitness of the hackney carriage vehicle, namely the approved testing station (“ATS”) scheme, which the Council’s elected members resolved to adopt for that purpose and which Ms Griffin subsequently confirmed would apply.

7(B)(iv)
Three principles apply.
1.
First, a person whose satisfaction a statute requires must form it upon the relevant material, which includes any assessment that the Council has itself designated for that purpose, and must give reasons if he declines to be satisfied in the face of it.
2.
Secondly, a licensing authority which has told those it licenses, by clear and unqualified published statements and by consistent practice, how a question will be determined is expected to follow that procedure, and may depart from it only if it identifies the departure and gives clear reasons for it; that is the expectation upon which I rely at paragraph 7(A)(iv).
3.
Thirdly, an authority must not adopt inconsistent positions on the same question without explanation: it cannot treat an inspection at an approved testing station as the means by which compliance with its Policy is assessed, as it has stated in terms, and then disregard the result of that inspection without saying why.
7(B)(v)
The Constitution identifies who may take the relevant decisions. Clause 21.1.1 allocates to the Taxi and Regulatory Committee “all matters relating to the grant, refusal, issue, revocation, cancellation and suspension of licences or permits” in respect of, among others, Hackney Carriage vehicles (paragraph 7(A)(ix)). Clause 19.7.1 applies where a function has been delegated to an officer, and provides that the decision “may be taken in the name of (but not necessarily personally by)” the delegated officer, and that:
“The Officer with delegated powers can delegate to a third party unless that Officer is specifically restricted from doing so relevant to that specific power.”

That clause presupposes a function delegated to an officer. I have identified no delegation to any officer of the functions in section 68 (paragraph 7(A)(iv)).

GROUND 5Continuing refusal to give effect to the certificate of an approved testing station, contrary to the Council’s own scheme, statements and the method of verification adopted by its elected members
7(B)(vi)
On 8 October 2026, two days after the suspension, HV0053 was tested at an approved testing station. The certificate (Enclosure K) records that the vehicle:
“passed the test and is in a safe and satisfactory condition to be used as a Hackney Carriage/Private Hire Vehicle in the Monmouthshire County Council area”.

No defect is recorded against any of items (a) to (h), and the certificate expires on 8 April 2027. The inspection schedule against which the station examines vehicles includes the rooflight (Enclosure M), and the certificate accordingly records no defect in respect of it. I supplied the certificate to PTU and Licensing on 8 October 2026 (section 5.2, item 5).

7(B)(vii)
On the Council’s own assertions (made by Ms Griffin) (paragraphs 7(B)(ii) and 7(B)(iii)), the test of October 2026 was the inspection at which compliance with the 2026 Policy arrangements fell to be assessed. Mr Watts himself identified it as such. The Council has given no reason whatsoever for treating an inspection at the approved testing station as insufficient to verify compliance with the 2026 Policy. The Council has accepted the station’s certificates for this vehicle since the licence was first granted, and the Council’s own newsletter states that the approved garage may fail a vehicle on the roof sign. The schedule applied by the station includes the rooflight (paragraph 7(B)(vi)).
7(B)(viii)
The notice and the later communications give no reason why the certificate does not answer the question of fitness. They record no consideration of it, and the letter of 19 October 2026 continues to require presentation of the vehicle for inspection and test notwithstanding that no officer has been identified as authorised to carry out that inspection and test.
7(B)(ix)
Section 68 vests the satisfaction in “such authorised officer or constable”. For the reasons at paragraph 7(A)(iv), no written authorisation of Mr Watts, or of any officer, has been identified. If the Council contends that the function is delegated to officers, clause 19.7.1 permits the decision to be taken in the name of the delegated officer through arrangements with a third party, and on that contention the certificate is the Council’s own exercise of the function and cannot be treated as without effect. If, as the absence of any published delegation indicates, the function is not delegated to officers, it is reserved to the Taxi and Regulatory Committee by clause 21.1.1, and no officer has power to maintain the suspension or to decline to be satisfied. The result is anomalous: the Council’s own scheme designates the certificate as the means by which compliance and fitness are assessed and confirmed, and that it is the lawful basis upon which a licence is granted, renewed and retained.
7(B)(x)
The requirement that HV0053 be presented again to an authorised officer therefore has no footing in the Council’s published scheme, and no lawful basis has been identified for the continued refusal to be satisfied as to fitness. The continuation of the suspension is unlawful on this further ground, independently of Grounds 1 to 4.

8Alternative remedy and urgency

8.1
I have considered whether any alternative remedy is available. There is no statutory right of appeal against a suspension under section 68. A right of appeal to the magistrates’ court arises only if the licence is deemed revoked upon the expiry of the two-month period, when subsections (2) and (3) of section 60 apply. Judicial review is the procedure by which a section 68 suspension may be challenged, (R (Wilcock) v Lancaster City Council [2013] EWHC 1231 (Admin); affirmed [2014] LLR 388 (CA)), and interim relief is available on such a claim. The inspection route described in paragraph 7(A)(x) is not an alternative remedy either.
8.2
The matter is urgent. The suspension is immediate and open-ended and has prevented me from working, and it remains in force for reasons which I am unable to reconcile with the statutory scheme. The consequences, including the termination by PTU of contract YGY-4 following its notice of 23 October 2026 and the inability to earn through my other trading income, are set out in section 5 and in the schedule at paragraph 9.3. This work is my family’s principal source of income, and my two children, both under the age of 7, depend upon it. The loss of that income since 6 October 2026 has placed my family under severe financial pressure and has caused me considerable stress and anxiety, which will continue for so long as the suspension remains in place and which, as I am a litigant in person, will be the more prolonged. This letter is sent 27 days after the date of the notice because I am without legal representation and have been obliged to research the law and the Council’s documents unaided. On the statutory timetable, the licence would be deemed revoked on or about 6 December 2026 (the notice states that the licence “may” be deemed revoked, whereas section 68 provides that it “shall” be so deemed). I therefore request a reply within the abridged period stated at the head of this letter, and I will proceed to issue the claim promptly if the reply does not resolve the matter.
8.3
I have also considered the observation in Wilcock that the licence holder could have complied first and challenged the decision subsequently. That course has not been open to me: under the established framework, and on the terms of the notice, no lawful means of compliance has been identified. I have taken adequate step to mitigate the matter at hand by obtaining a fresh vehicle fit certificate in accordance with subsequent advices provided to me during the course of dealing with this matter, however, suspension is still not lifted.
